Everyone measures the host.

Nobody, in any country, publishes how long a domain registrar takes to act after a notice about child sexual abuse material. The content moves between hosts. The domain is what users follow. That is the gap.

02

What is missing, precisely

Two things, and only two.

One. Registrar response time. After a valid notice, how long until the domain is suspended or the material is unreachable, per registrar, over time. Rough bands are enough. Nothing like it exists in any jurisdiction.

Two. Re-registration. Whether the same operators register again after a takedown, and with which registrars. The Dutch authority records in its own 2025 annual report that removed material was in a number of cases re-posted, and says it will watch how that develops. Nobody publishes the registrar side of that pattern.

Why the domain and not the host. Content migrates between hosting providers cheaply and often. The domain name is the persistent part: it is what a user types, what a link points at, and the cheapest route back online after a takedown.
03

Why closing it would work

Four findings from published data. Together they make the case that measuring this layer would change behaviour, and that it is a tractable thing to do.

87% → 40%

Measurement changes behaviour, and its absence changes it back

When the Dutch monitor was running and providers knew they were being measured and named, 87 per cent of this material was removed within 24 hours of a notice. In the following measurement, after engagement lapsed, that fell to 40 per cent, with 56 per cent still online after 48 hours. Nothing about the technology changed. The attention did.

TU Delft with EOKM, CSAM Hosting Monitor, measurements of December 2021 to January 2022 and November to December 2022.

9 providers, 78%

Harm concentrates in a very small number of actors

Across 595 providers worldwide, nine of them, holding just 5.5 per cent of the material, account for 78 per cent of all the days that material stayed reachable. Two providers alone account for 57 per cent. The largest providers by volume are among the fastest to remove; the damage sits with a handful of mid-sized ones that do not act.

Computed by Custorian from the published dataset of Project Arachnid’s 2025 ESP report. C3P publishes the distribution; this concentration cut is ours.

1,252

The registrar layer is just as concentrated, which makes it tractable

There are 3,322 ICANN accreditations, but one company can hold many. They resolve to roughly a thousand operators, and a single operator holds 1,252 accreditations on its own. Changing what this layer commits to does not mean moving three thousand companies. It means moving a few.

Custorian analysis of the IANA registrar registry and ICANN monthly registry reports.

93%

A deadline with an enforcer behind it produces compliance

The Dutch authority issued roughly 180 removal orders for terrorist content in 2025 under a one-hour statutory deadline. Every order was complied with, and 93 per cent of material came down inside the hour. The same authority operates a 12-hour statutory deadline for child sexual abuse material. Where the hour was missed, it referred the provider to the authority in the country where it is established.

ATKM, Jaarverslag 2025.

Put together: being measured and named improves removal, most of the harm sits with a handful of companies, the registrar layer is concentrated enough that a handful is all you would need to reach, and a published deadline with consequences attached works. Every one of those findings comes from the hosting layer or from statute. None of it has ever been applied to registrars, because nobody has the data.

Why this matters for the layer below. The population is small, it is stable across years and across countries, and independent sources converge on it without coordinating. That is what makes a register of this kind work, and it is exactly the exercise that has never been run for the companies that sell the domain names.
One row needs reading carefully. Stark Industries removed 100 per cent of the material measured within 24 hours. It is separately designated by the European Union on grounds that have nothing to do with that measurement. Those are two different facts about two different things, and neither implies the other. We keep them in the same table because hiding either would be worse.
05

What we are building

A public accountability record at the registrar layer: what each registrar has committed to in writing about child sexual abuse material, set against measured performance wherever a credible third party has published it.

We have read the binding documents behind 2,752 of the world’s 3,322 ICANN accreditations, across 78 countries. Of those:

202name child sexual abuse material as its own prohibited category
2,529have catch-all wording only, with no child-specific term at all
1,401name DNS abuse, but not this

It is not a detection operation and it is not a list of accusations. It records what a company has published about itself, and every external figure is attributed to whoever published it. We never assert that a registrar hosts abuse material, because that cannot be established at this layer.

06

What would help

In order. A pointer to a person or a dataset is worth as much to us as data.

  1. Registrar response times. After a valid notice, how long until the domain is suspended or the material is gone, per registrar. Rough bands are fine. Even a small sample is more than exists today.
  2. Re-registration patterns. Whether the same operators register again after a takedown, and with which registrars.
  3. Which registrars recur most often in your reporting workload, in aggregate. Not a characterisation of any company, just which names keep coming back.
  4. Or simply: does anyone track this informally? If registrar response or re-registration is watched anywhere, even if never published, we would like to know. If the answer is that nobody does, that is itself a finding we would like confirmed.
07

What we will never ask for

  • No case material.
  • No URLs.
  • No imagery of any kind.
  • Nothing identifying a child or an offender.
  • No characterisation of any company by you.

Aggregate is fine. Historical is fine. Banded is fine. Nothing needs to be live.
