Three UX rules that apply everywhere

  1. The reason is always visible. A score, a match or a tie shows the fields behind it on the same screen, never behind a model.
  2. Absence is shown, not hidden. A metric that does not yet exist appears as a labelled gap (the Layer 4 hook from the Registrar Index scope).
  3. Nothing is a ranking until it is verified. Unverified data is displayed as profiles and comparable fields; ranks appear only where the evidence label is Reviewed or better.
02

Intermediary Accountability Index

The Index scores every company that sits between an abuser and a child on the open web, on how it handles child sexual abuse material, from public evidence first and partner data later.

It generalises the Registrar Accountability Index one-pager (17 Sep 2026) from one layer to six, keeping its four-band scoring logic and its rule that version one needs nobody’s permission.

Layers in scope

Each layer is a separate league table with its own metrics, because a registrar and a payment processor cannot be compared on one number. A parent company with entities in several layers gets one entity page that rolls the layers up.

LayerWhoMeasurable from public data nowNeeds a partner

Hosting providersASN holders; C3P 2025 ESP dataset (2,752 accreditations resolved)Named in Arachnid data, jurisdiction of legal entity vs server location, terms, abuse contact, repeat appearance across yearsRemoval time per notice, recidivism (C3P, IWF, SafeNet BG, ATKM)

Scoring model

Four bands per layer, each 0 to 25, summed to a 0 to 100 layer score. Bands 1 to 3 are computable today; band 4 shows as a labelled gap until a data partner signs.

  1. Policy transparency. Does the published contract name child sexual abuse material specifically, name a reporting route, and commit to a response time.
  2. Public posture. Abuse contact published and reachable, transparency report exists, participates in a recognised scheme (IWF membership, INHOPE, DSA trusted-flagger route, ICANN DNS abuse commitments).
  3. Structure and jurisdiction. Legal entity identifiable, jurisdiction compellable, ownership cluster disclosed; penalised for shell structures found by the ASN clustering work.
  4. Response (coming soon). Median time from notice to action, share of notices actioned, recidivism.

Every band publishes its rubric, the evidence for each point and the date checked. A change in a company’s score generates a public changelog entry with the document that caused it. Companies improve their score only by changing a public document or a measured behaviour, never by contacting Custorian.

Data sources at launch

Registrar audit dataset (Registrar Watch), ASN clustering and Censys research access, C3P Arachnid 2025 ESP dataset and the 2021 named cohort, IWF annual hosting data, ATKM and EOKM hosting monitors, ICANN accreditation list, company registers (CVR, Companies House, OpenCorporates), Watchtower scans, FinCEN notice.

Partner data (C3P, IWF, SafeNet Bulgaria, Quad9, Danish SIC) would fill band 4 under written agreements, aggregate only, nothing that identifies a report.

What it is not

Not a blocklist, not a takedown service, not a certification. The Index is the public scorecard; CDSW certification is the platform layer’s verified tier. Custorian takes no money from any entity that appears in it.

03

Index UX and UI

The Index is a reading product before it is a ranking product.

The person arriving is a journalist, a regulator’s analyst, a hosting company’s trust and safety lead, or a funder checking a claim. Each of them wants to get from a layer to a named company to the evidence in under a minute, and to leave knowing exactly why a score is what it is.

The league table is the front door, the entity page is where the argument is made, and every other screen is one click from the entity page: the cluster view shows who else sits under the same owner, the dispute flow is the entity’s right of reply, and the download screen gives the same evidence to anyone who wants to reuse it.

Flows

  1. Look up an entity. Search or browse the layer table, open the entity, read the bands top to bottom, open the chips that matter, download the pack.
  2. Check a cluster. From any entity, open the cluster view, see the siblings, return to whichever sibling matters.
  3. Claim and correct. An entity representative opens the claim flow, verifies a work email at the entity’s domain, submits a correction with a document. The submission appears on the entity page as Declared within a day, and moves to Reviewed only after a named reviewer signs it off.
  4. Reuse. A journalist copies the badge or the API call; a researcher downloads the CSV. Both carry the methodology version so an old quote can be traced.

Visual rules

  • Brand purple for structure and links; gold only for the Custorian certification seal, never for scores.
  • No red, amber or green anywhere a score is shown unless the score is Verified. Before that, bands are drawn in a single neutral tone with the number.
  • Mobile first, with a 16 px gutter; the league table collapses to cards on a phone, with the four bands stacked.
  • WCAG 2.2 AA: every band and label readable without colour, every chip reachable by keyboard, every table sortable by screen reader.
  • Plain language at a reading age of around 14; every technical term links to the methodology page.

Ecosystem Map: two views, one registry

Who is in the child safety ecosystem and how are they tied to each other; and what does the abuse material actually travel through on its way from an uploader to a screen.

Those are two different pictures of two different populations, so the product has two views, but one entity registry, one evidence store and one set of labels underneath.

View 1: Organisations and ties

This is the dossier drawn. Nodes are organisations: hotlines, NGOs, industry bodies, vendors, funders, regulators, law enforcement units, academic centres. Edges are ties, each with a type and an evidence chip.

Tie typeExampleEvidence needed
FundsA foundation grant to a hotlineAnnual report, grant database, 990 or equivalent
GovernsA board seat, a trustee, a shared directorRegister filing, published board list
SuppliesA vendor licensing a hash list or classifier to an NGOContract announcement, product documentation
Member ofAn organisation inside an alliance or associationPublic member list
Shares staffA person who moved between two nodes within the periodLinkedIn, press release, staff page
Reports toA hotline submitting to a clearinghousePublished process, MoU

Each node carries: name, type, country, founding year, headline funding sources, and the same Declared, Reviewed, Verified label on every fact. Each edge carries: type, direction, first seen, last confirmed, and its evidence.

View 2: Infrastructure chain

This is the Index’s population drawn as a supply chain. The layers are the same six as the Index. Nodes are companies placed in their layer. Edges are dependencies: this hosting company resolves through this registrar, this platform pays through this processor. The Arachnid concentration figures (2021 cohort, 2025 concentration) attach as weights, so the drawing can show that most reported material sits under a handful of names.

A node that appears in both views (a hosting company that is also a member of an industry body) is one record with two placements, so a click in either view lands on the same entity page as the Index.

What the Map is for

  • A journalist can trace a funding line from a technology company through a foundation to a hotline in three clicks, with the evidence for each step.
  • A regulator can see how much of the reported material sits with entities in one jurisdiction.
  • A small NGO can see who else works in its corner and who funds them, which is the bridge to the Matchmaker.
  • Custorian can show, rather than assert, the concentration and the conflicts that the book describes.

What it is not

It is not a social graph of people. Individuals appear only as the reason for a “shares staff” tie between two organisations, named only where already public, and never with a page of their own. It is not a live network scan; View 2 is built from published analyses and Custorian’s own mapping sheets, and says so.

Flows

  1. Follow the money. Search a funder, open its side panel, expand “funds” ties, click a recipient, expand again. Each step adds nodes without redrawing the whole graph.
  2. Who touches this material. Open the infrastructure view with weights on, filter to one jurisdiction, read the lanes top to bottom.
  3. Prove a connection. Open the path view, pick two entities, read the chain, copy it with sources into a document.
  4. Correct the map. Any node’s side panel has the same claim and dispute flow as the Index; a disputed tie shows a marker until reviewed.

Visual rules

  • The opening view must be readable on a laptop without zooming: a curated set, not the whole registry.
  • Colour carries type only, never judgement. No node is drawn as good or bad.
  • On a phone, the graph is replaced by a list: an entity, its ties grouped by type, and the path view as a vertical strip. The graph is a desktop feature and the product says so rather than shipping a cramped version.
  • Every graph state has a plain-text equivalent (the side panel and the path strip), so the product is usable with a screen reader and quotable in print.
  • Same purple and gold rules as the Index, same type scale, same 16 px gutter.
06

Funding Matchmaker: three entry points

The Funding Commons concept note made into a product: a two-sided registry where funders and small child safety organisations describe themselves once, in the same schema, and are matched with reasons they can see.

It has three ways in, because the three kinds of user arrive with different questions.

Entry point 1: the funder

A foundation, a corporate giving team or a public grant programme. The question is “who is doing credible work in this corner that we cannot find through our usual channels.” The funder publishes a profile (themes, geographies, ticket size range, what it will not fund, decision calendar) and sees a ranked list of entities with the reason for each match written out.

Entry point 2: the entity

A small NGO, hotline, research group or a new consortium. The question is “who would fund this and when do they decide.” The entity fills the profile schema from the concept note (mission, activities, outcomes with the Declared, Reviewed, Verified label, budget band, data holdings, governance) and sees funders whose calendars and criteria fit, with the same visible reasons, plus a calendar of upcoming decision windows.

Entry point 3: the consortium builder

Anyone, including Custorian, trying to assemble a group for a larger bid (an EU call, a multi-country programme). The question is “who has the missing piece.” The builder describes the bid (call, deadline, required capabilities, countries) and the Matchmaker proposes entities that cover each gap, drawn from the same registry, with a workspace to invite them and track who has said yes.

What it is not

It is not a grant portal: no applications are submitted through it and no money moves. It is not a rating of organisations: the labels say how well a claim is evidenced, not how good the organisation is. It is not open to the public in the way the Index and Map are: profiles are visible to registered users of the other side, and the entity chooses what is public.

The only product with logged-in users on both sides, so onboarding, trust and privacy decide whether anyone comes back.

The design goal: a small NGO with one part-time person can publish a credible profile in under an hour, and a funder can find three organisations it did not know in under ten minutes.

The rest of the kit

  • Label badge. Declared, Reviewed, Verified, identical to the other products.
  • Calendar window. A dated block with funder name, themes, ticket band and a link, in the funder’s own colour only within the calendar.

Flows

  1. Entity onboarding. Register, confirm the organisation from the registry, complete the profile in steps (basics, activities, outcomes, budget band, data holdings, governance), preview, publish. Matches appear the same day.
  2. Funder discovery. Register, set criteria and calendar, read the match list, save five, open their profiles, export the shortlist with reasons.
  3. Consortium assembly. Create a bid, list the required capabilities, accept the proposed entities or search for others, invite, watch the grid fill, export the roster and the notes.
  4. Evidence review. An entity uploads an outcome document; it appears as Declared; a reviewer checks it against the outcome template; it becomes Reviewed and the entity is notified.

Visual and trust rules

  • Nothing about an entity is shown to a funder before the entity publishes it, and the preview shows exactly what will be visible.
  • Matching never uses a hidden weight. If a criterion counts, it is listed on the card; if it is not listed, it did not count.
  • No funder is ranked or scored; funders are matched, not judged.
  • The calendar and the match list both work as plain lists on a phone; the consortium grid is desktop first with a list fallback.
  • Same brand rules as the other two products, with gold only on the certification seal and never on a match.
  • WCAG 2.2 AA throughout; the stepped form saves after every field so a dropped connection loses nothing.
08

Shared architecture

One system with three front doors. The Index creates the entity registry and the evidence store, the Map reuses both and adds ties, the Matchmaker reuses all of it and adds profiles and accounts.

The products only ever read and write through the shared services. The entity registry is the one record per organisation; the evidence store holds every archived source with its capture date and its label; the review queue is where a named reviewer moves a fact from Declared to Reviewed and the changelog records it. Underneath, the data layer has three kinds of input: public sources that Custorian imports and archives, partner data that is not yet available, and what users type into the Matchmaker or the claim flows.

Services and technology

  • Web app. One codebase, three route groups, a shared component library (band bar, evidence chip, label badge, changelog, node chip, match card). Server-rendered pages for everything public so the Index and Map are indexable and fast; client rendering only for the graph and the consortium grid.
  • Database. One relational database (Postgres) with row-level access rules: public rows for the Index and Map, role-scoped rows for Matchmaker profiles. Supabase fits, and Custorian already has an account.
  • Archive. Every evidence URL is captured to object storage at the time of review, with a hash, so a score can be defended after the source page changes.
  • Review tooling. The review queue is an internal page in the same app, not a separate system; reviewer identity comes from the same accounts.
  • Open data. Nightly export of the Index and the public parts of the Map as CSV and JSON under CC BY 4.0, a read-only JSON API with the methodology version in every response, and the embeddable badge served from the same endpoint.
  • Accounts. Email verified at the organisation’s domain; no social login; two roles per user at most.
  • Hosting. Vercel for the app and Supabase for data and storage, both already in use, with a documented export path so the whole system can move if the board decides to.

Governance built in. The seven governance rules from the Funding Commons note become code where they can: an entity’s profile is invisible until published; every fact carries a label; every change has a reviewer; the methodology is versioned and public; the data is open under one licence; disputes are visible on the page while open; and nothing in any product is a ranking until it is Verified.

09

Build sequence

The order follows the dependency. Each gate is a decision, not a date: the next phase starts only when the gate’s criteria are met, and the board sees the evidence at the gate.

The Index creates the registry and evidence store that everything else needs, and it is the product with material already in hand (the registrar one-pager scope, the Arachnid analyses, the mapping sheets). The Map comes second because it reuses the registry and the dossier is already written. The Matchmaker comes last because it is the only product with accounts on both sides and it needs the registry to be populated before a funder sees anything worth matching.

Effort and money

Across the twelve months this is roughly one developer full time throughout, one designer at half time for the first two months of each product, one researcher full time from November, and reviewer time that can come from advisors at first and must be paid for by the Matchmaker phase.

The Funding Commons note’s EUR 187,000 covered the Matchmaker alone; the full sequence is closer to twice that if the developer and researcher are paid at Danish rates. The foundation and Index phases are the part to raise for first, because they are the part with material in hand and the clearest public output.

What can slip without harm

The infrastructure view of the Map and the consortium workspace of the Matchmaker can be pushed out without breaking anything else. The response time band of the Index depends on partner data and stays marked “coming soon” until a partner signs, whatever the calendar says.

Open decisions for the founder and board

The choices this document does not make. Each has a recommendation, and each changes something above if decided the other way.

  1. Is the Index a ranking or a register?A register with scores until every band on a layer is Verified, then a ranking for that layer only.Deciding on a ranking from day one changes the league table’s default sort and raises the legal exposure of every score.
  2. Who reviews?A named reviewer panel of three (a board member, Dr Alison McClymont or another clinical advisor for anything touching child welfare claims, and one external, for example from the academic contacts in the network), with initials on every changelog entry.Deciding that Custorian staff review alone is cheaper and weaker.
  3. Do entities get notice before a page goes live?Yes, 14 days, with the claim flow open during the notice period.Deciding no is faster and invites the first dispute to be about process rather than facts.
  4. Which layer after registrars?Hosting, because the Arachnid concentration figures already exist and make the second layer cheap.Deciding on platforms first is more visible and much harder to evidence.
  5. Individuals on the Map.Never as nodes, only as the reason for a shares staff tie, and only where already public.Deciding to show people changes the product into something Custorian has criticised others for.
  6. Matchmaker fees.Free for entities always; a membership fee for funders from Gate 3, priced so ten funders cover the reviewer panel.Deciding on free for everyone leaves review unpaid and the labels unreliable.
  7. Build in house or with a studio?One developer in house from the foundation phase, because the three products share one codebase and the knowledge needs to stay. A studio for the design system and the graph view only.
  8. Certification seal.Keep the gold seal out of all three products until the Index has a Verified layer; then a certified entity is one whose Index score is Verified and above a published threshold.Deciding to issue seals earlier would tie the brand’s only gold to an unverified number.
  9. Co-authorship and the book.The book, the dossier and the Map tell the same story. Launch the Map’s organisations view alongside the book’s publication, with the book’s sources as the Map’s first evidence set, so each sells the other.
  10. What to fund first.Raise for the foundation and the registrar Index as one package (roughly the first five months), because it is the smallest thing with a public result, and use the live Index to raise for the rest.
